   ## What did I learn?
   
   ### Minima naturalia — a lower bound on size
   
   Duhem (p. 37) presents the Aristotelian–scholastic doctrine of *minima
   naturalia*: the substantial form of a natural body imposes a **determinate
   lower bound** on the quantity of matter that can still instantiate that form.
   Below that threshold the body cannot persist as the same kind of thing.
   
   The locus in Aquinas for the **determinate-quantity claim** is:
   
   > *In Physicorum* I, lect. 9, nn. 65–66, 68
   
   There Aquinas argues (commenting on Aristotle *Physics* I.4) that natural
   bodies have both upper and lower bounds on their quantity, fixed by their
   substantial form.
   
   ### Water → air: substantial generation, not mere phase shift
   
   The stock example — that sufficiently rarefied water *becomes* air — is
   **Aristotelian** in origin (*Physics* IV; *De generatione et corruptione* I).
   Aquinas comments on it in two places:
   
   - *In Physicorum* IV, lect. 8, n. 493
   - *In De generatione et corruptione* I, lect. 14, n. 100
   
   In both passages the change is treated as **substantial generation**: the
   original substantial form is destroyed and a new one comes to be. This is not
   analogous to a continuous phase transition; it is a *qualitative* threshold
   with no surviving substrate-identity across it.
   
   A related passage on rarefaction and condensation having **finite limits**:
   
   > *In Physicorum* IV, lect. 14, n. 554
   
   (Aquinas denies that rarefaction can proceed without bound — again because the
   form sets the range.)
   
   ### Wine diluted into water
   
   Another canonical minima-naturalia example — wine disappearing into a large
   body of water — appears in:
   
   > *In De generatione et corruptione* I, lect. 17
   
   There the dissolved wine is said to lose its specific form below the minimum
   quantity; it does not persist in attenuated form, it simply ceases to *be* wine.
   
   ### What Aquinas adds to Aristotle
   
   Aquinas does not merely repeat Aristotle. He sharpens the doctrine by grounding
   the lower bound explicitly in the **metaphysics of substantial form**: the form
   is not just a pattern but an intrinsic principle that requires a certain
   quantity for its actualization. This is a tighter, more explicit argument than
   anything in the *Physics* text itself.
   
   **Important caveat:** No verbatim Aquinas sentence of the form "diluted water
   becomes air" has been located. The formulation in Duhem (p. 37) is Duhem's
   paraphrase. Always cite the Marietti/Leonine loci above if writing this up more
   formally.
   
   ## What do I think about it?
   
   The doctrine is interesting as a **structural analogy** — not an anticipation —
   to two ideas in modern physics:
   
   1. **Effective field theory / renormalization**: every effective description has
      an energy scale (a *cutoff*) below which it breaks down and must be replaced
      by a new description. The minima naturalia play the same architectural role:
      they are scale-dependent validity conditions on a description. This is the
      strongest formal parallel.
   
   2. **Phase transitions**: the water → air case superficially resembles a
      first-order transition (a sharp qualitative change at a threshold). But the
      analogy is shallow: Aristotle/Aquinas attribute the change to form-destruction
      and substantial generation, not to the statistical mechanics of a free-energy
      landscape. Calling it an "anticipation of phase transitions" overstates the
      case.
   
   The deeper question the doctrine raises is whether **thermodynamics presupposes
   its ontology**. Thermodynamic quantities like temperature and entropy are
   defined for systems with many degrees of freedom; they become ill-defined for
   arbitrarily small systems. The minima naturalia, framed in terms of substantial
   form, are asking a related question: at what scale does a description fail, and
   what replaces it? Medieval natural philosophy and modern physics ask the same
   structural question by completely different means.
